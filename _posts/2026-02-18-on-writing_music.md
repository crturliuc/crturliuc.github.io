This post is addressed to people that would like to write rock/metal music but have no experience in writing music. Note that I am also a hobbyist, and a lazy one at that, so take everything with a grain of salt. 
Despite my process being rudimentary, I believe it can still offer a few initial directions and help with complete beginner painpoints.
The following text is a verbatim email sent to a friend addressing this topic (except some markdown formatting).

"Hi Piyush,

Hope you don't mind the email instead of signal, I can't type comfortably on mobile longer texts. I can describe to you only my approach to writing music, as it is the only one I know. :) 

I always start with the "riff", or what in a normal 5-piece band would be the rhythm guitar (so not the vox, not the bass, not the lead guitar, nor the drums). For very few riffs sometimes I started with a drum pattern as well, but immediately after came the "riff". At this stage I place no constraints on myself, anything goes. Once I have one riff I really like and I think can be the kernel of a song, there are two options: 1) I try to extend it somehow, a common pattern is 1-1-1-2, where 1 is the original riff and 2 is either completely new, or similar to 1 but transposed, or with the ending changed. You can see this for example in Pestilential Chaos (of Poliel) (played on bass but it doesn't matter that much)
```
1 = A 0 0 0 0 0 2 3 2
2 = D 2 3 2 0 1 0 
    A             3 2
```
(here 1 and 2 are similar at the end, usually it's either the beginning or the end)
or a pattern of 1-2-1-3 where 2 and 3 are similar in "my" chorus of Heretics:
```
1 =
A   2 3 2
E 0        0 0

2 = 
A 5 4 3 0 2
E           3

3 = 
A 5 4 3(bend down and up twice)
```
Indeed here 2 and 3 share the beginning chromatic descent 5 4 3 = D C# C.

Option 2) is to create an independent riff that doesn't necessarily share context with riff 1. For example the middle part (slow) in Winterblast isn't super "connected" to the fast part that comes before and after it. Here it's up to you to decide if riff 1 goes together with riff 2, or you need riff 3 or some long note to "glue" them.

Anyway, now you have a "verse" and "chorus" or part 1 and part 2 that are your bigger units to play with in the song structure. Ideally, you write at least another one, a "bridge", maybe a "solo", maybe an intro and/or an outro or whatever you want. But even if you don't have the complete thing in mind, you need to check for each of these parts:
1) time signature - does the melody fit into 4/4 (all my songs are 4/4, I recommend to start from there, especially if you will have tremolo riffs, I have no idea how tremolo could sound in 6/8 or 3/4, I need to actually check :) ). Time signatures other than 4/4, 3/4, 6/8 are tricky for a beginner, I would not touch them for the first drafts of songs.
2) what is the tempo, i.e. X BPM, X=?. This is hard and tedious in the beginning, you will gaslight yourself often, but eventually your answer will crystalize into something acceptable/stable. I often prefer to use the half-tempo for speedy tracks, e.g. I consider Chased to be 145 BPM, but probably a "normal" musician would consider it 290 BPM. That doesn't matter, it just changes half notes into quarter, quarter in eighths and so on, and doubles the number of measures. I recommend using multiples of 5 when adjusting the metronome, unless you are really really pedantic.

At this stage, create a "metronome" drum track in Reaper that is just K K K K, or S K K K, or K S K S, or K K S2, S2 = snare half note, the others are quarter notes all. The last pattern is basically 95% of the drums in Angelwings and Ravenclaws, and I also used it in Chased.

Record yourself or program the melody while listening (but obviously not capturing) the metronome track. If playing, realize your rhythm SUCKS, and you are always early or late. That's ok, practice practice, practice until you get decent at it, and you get a take you like.

Repeat for all parts, it's easier of course if everything same is the same time signature (mine are only 4/4 so far) and tempo (e.g. Chased, counter-example Winterblast where middle is slower, and not by half or by quarter, it's like 100 BPM slow vs 300 BPM fast, so you cannot put them in the same time "grid"or "region" or whatever you want to call it).

When (not if, when) you get stuck on how to glue things together it's time to think if you want to write in a particular key. Candidates can be found in the lowest (and most frequent) notes in the chorus/verse. For example, I consider Winterblast to be in A minor, and Chased to be in B Phrygian (I won't go into modes here, but basically it's not A minor because A is missing from the A B C D etc. scale, and the true low note which is the open E on the guitar is more of a drone than a "true" root). You may feel too constrained, or you may feel more sane that you have a more limited range of notes/chords to use, this is totally up to you.

Ok, now you have all or most of the rhythm guitar written. Play/record/program clean guitar, even if it sounds like surf rock or whatever. Then bring in the effect chain and play with the million options until you go mad and give up and settle for something that "works" for you.

Next step is to write drums. My approach to drums is to write boring drums when interesting things are happening on the guitar and interesting drums when the guitar are not particularly dynamic. It's easier to tie a riff or a part of a riff to the same drum pattern. For fast parts you definitely want a pattern to copy paste, I used for example:
```
H H
S S
K K
``` 
etc. with variations between H = hihat to C = crash and R = ride in Chased on different measures and
```
H   H
K S K S
```
etc. as the "base" for the fast part in Winterblast.

Next I write bass, which again can be boring (root notes), or simple (arpeggiated power chords, i.e. root-perfect fifth-octaves), or spicy and beyond. Even for fast parts I downpick everything on bass, as I don't think tremolo picking bass works. I would rather have a slower bass tempo with a clean sound than a mudfest. Even if bass is quite percussive, it still holds a part of melody, so now you enter the territory of harmony (multiple  "voices"  = instruments), and voice leading, and other music theory terms I won't pretend to understand well at all. Just play the two together with some preliminary mix and see if the two instruments work on top of each other.

Next I write synths/leads, and here the harmony part is even more important if you go beyond  "simple" into the "spicy and beyond" territory.

Last step is vocals, lyrics etc.which are based on the "vibe" of your song and the "vision" of your project/album/whatever. For example, the reason why I made "Thyr Tellann" is because 1) I love Malazan Book of the Fallen and 2) Caladan Brood (the band) never made a second album so far and also newer Summoning wasn't enough, the last album of theirs I truly adore is Let Mortal Heroes Sing Your Fame, probably my favorite tied with Minas Morgul. :)

Write a lot of different stuff, create cover songs and study their instruments, but most importantly enjoy the process, find the joy in expressing yourself through music. I never programmed tremolo riffs, not sure how well that works, if you want I can get you started on how to practice them on guitar or play them for you. Don't be afraid to take inspiration from bands you love, e.g. my Heretics riffs are obviously inspired by [Electric Funeral](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZyEUk1ZJLn4) and [Solitude](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UZjuzPU9UE4) and that's fine. :)

SORRY for the massive wall of text, feel free to ask questions about any part, and also share your perspective on writing/analyzing music. 

P.S. If you are talking a walk through the snow and/or enjoy "God is an Astronaut"-like bands, check out this Romanian post-rock album, it's such a vibe: https://aflmsmp.bandcamp.com/album/la-vale

Best,

Calin
